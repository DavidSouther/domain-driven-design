# Papers: Matching LLM model selection to task complexity

## Findings

### 1. When smaller models suffice vs. larger models are necessary

The dominant empirical framing is the LLM cascade: route a query to the cheapest model first and escalate only when a confidence or quality check fails. FrugalGPT shows this strategy can match a top-tier model's accuracy at up to 98% lower cost, or beat it by several points at equal cost, because a large share of real queries do not need the strongest model [1]. Surveys of small-model deployment confirm this is not a niche trick: small models remain competitive whenever computational cost, latency, or deployment footprint is constrained, and organizations routinely under-use them relative to their actual capability [5]. Empirical benchmarking of small code-generation models finds accuracy gains from added parameters flatten well below frontier scale — once a small model clears a capability threshold for a task family, further scale yields marginal returns, so the "necessary" tier is task-family-specific rather than a fixed size cutoff [7]. Conversely, a body of work on emergent abilities documents specific tasks (multi-step arithmetic, certain instruction-following and symbolic-reasoning benchmarks) where performance is flat and near-random below some scale threshold, then rises sharply above it — meaning for those task families, no amount of prompting or cascading substitutes for a larger model [2].

### 2. Task-complexity dimensions correlating with model-tier requirements

Four dimensions recur across the literature:

- **Reasoning depth / multi-step inference.** Chain-of-thought prompting is itself an emergent-scale effect: it produces flat or even negative results below roughly 100B-parameter-class models and only reliably improves accuracy above that regime, because smaller models tend to break the reasoning chain or hallucinate intermediate steps rather than execute it [3]. Tasks requiring standard (non-chain) prompting show much shallower scaling curves than tasks requiring multi-step chains, so "how many dependent steps does the task require" is a strong predictor of whether a larger model is needed [3].
- **Symbolic/constraint-guided reasoning.** Recent comparative work finds larger models retain a distinct advantage specifically on constraint-satisfaction and rule-following reasoning, a narrower category than general reasoning, suggesting complexity should be decomposed rather than treated as one axis (general reasoning vs. constraint tracking scale differently) [search finding, corroborated across multiple 2025-2026 papers surfaced during this research].
- **Domain specificity and code generation.** Code-generation accuracy correlates strongly with parameter count up to a threshold, after which small and mid-sized specialized models perform competitively with much larger generalists on the same benchmark family, indicating domain-narrow tasks compress the tier requirement relative to open-domain tasks [7].
- **Generation vs. evaluation asymmetry.** It is commonly assumed that judging output is easier than producing it, but controlled studies find this does not hold uniformly: generation accuracy exceeds self-evaluation accuracy on most tested benchmarks, and a weaker model's grading signal degrades as the capability gap with the model it is judging widens [8], [9]. This means "reviewer" and "generator" roles cannot automatically be assigned to the same tier by assuming review is the easier task.

### 3. Cost-performance tradeoff patterns and when they matter most

Routing surveys formalize model selection as a joint cost-performance optimization rather than a single capability threshold, and identify query-difficulty variance as the key precondition: cascading and routing produce the largest savings when a workload mixes many easy queries with a minority of hard ones, and produce little benefit when all queries are uniformly difficult (nothing to route away from the expensive model) or uniformly easy (no need for the expensive model at all) [4]. Practitioner reporting converges on the same shape: diverting a learned share of traffic to smaller models can cut inference spend by roughly half to over 80% with small, controlled quality loss, and the effect is largest in high-volume, latency-sensitive, or classification-like workloads [13]. Recent routing research also shows that lightweight, low-overhead routers (e.g., similarity/kNN-based) can match or beat more complex learned routers, meaning the cost of building the router itself should be weighed against the savings it captures — over-engineering the routing layer can erode the benefit it is meant to produce [11].

### 4. Phase-based and role-based model selection

Distinct roles are treated in the literature and in practitioner architectures as warranting distinct tiers, not a single model for the whole pipeline. Multi-agent orchestration work assigns capability by function: a controller/orchestrator handling global state and decomposition, a planner/manager doing task breakdown and re-planning, and workers executing narrowly scoped subtasks — architectures reporting that insulating each worker to a single bounded tool call preserves accuracy even under heavy concurrent load, independent of which tier each role runs on [multi-agent orchestration findings, corroborated across several 2025-2026 architecture papers surfaced in this research]. One evolving-agent framework explicitly varies the model per role within the same pipeline — using a stronger model for meta-orchestration and judging while varying the execution-agent model across experiments — treating role-to-tier mapping as an experimental variable rather than a fixed assignment. The generation/evaluation asymmetry findings above reinforce that "planning" and "reviewing" should each be evaluated for their own complexity profile rather than inheriting the tier chosen for "doing" [8], [9].

### 5. Model routing, cascade, and stratification literature

The foundational formalization is the LLM cascade (query the cheapest model, escalate on low confidence) [1], with subsequent survey work organizing routing implementations into four families: similarity-based routing (route by query embedding proximity to known-easy/hard examples), supervised classifiers trained to predict which model tier suffices, reinforcement-learning-based routing policies, and generative/LLM-as-router methods that use a model to decide where to send the query [4]. A parallel line of work on collaborative (not purely competitive) small/large model systems treats the small model as a first-pass or verification layer rather than strictly a fallback, and frames the choice between "small models compete with large models" and "small models collaborate with large models" as two distinct design philosophies with different cost/quality curves [6]. Newer self-aware routing work extends the cascade with token-efficiency accounting, suggesting router quality should be measured jointly on accuracy and total tokens spent (including the discarded cheap-model attempt), not accuracy alone [12].

### Practitioner corroboration

Anthropic's own model-selection guidance mirrors the academic framing without hardcoding a fixed mapping: it recommends starting from either a fast/cheap model and upgrading only on demonstrated capability gaps, or starting from the most capable model for complex reasoning, scientific, or high-autonomy agentic work and downgrading once the workflow is well-understood — treating tier selection as an empirically-driven, benchmark-tested decision per use case rather than a static assignment, and explicitly calling out sub-agent tasks as suited to smaller/cheaper models within a larger pipeline [10].
